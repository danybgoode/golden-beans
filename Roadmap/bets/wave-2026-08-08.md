a rail and a front door — a PM can now *see* what their agent did. This wave is about what they can
*do*: the surfaces where the backend has long been able to act and the human has not.

## Decisions of record

- **#13 is bet first because it is the cheap follow-through on work already paid for.** The kit went
  from 1 component to **9** under `app-shell-and-agent-rail`, and `check-design-drift`'s `SWEPT_ROOTS`
  now covers `components/ui` and `components/product`. Adoption is guarded as it lands. Meanwhile
  adoption itself is still **2 of 26** routes — the kit grew, the app did not follow. Both #15 and
  #16 consume `ConfirmDialog` and `FormSection`; funding them without funding #13 means paying for
  those primitives twice, in two epics, in two shapes.

- **The three primitives ship as their own sprint, before any route conversion.** Named in #13's
  open risks and repeated here because it is the sequencing that matters: if the sweep stalls
  mid-wave, the primitives must already exist for #15 to start.

- **#15's appetite is M *because of a verification finding*, not optimism.** The pass against live
  `main` found `FLAG_CONTEXT_FIELDS` is a **closed 6-value enum** and `FlagClause` supports exactly
  **two** operators. The audit implied a general targeting builder — GrowthBook-shaped, unbounded.
  The real thing is a form over a 6×2 grid with server-side validation already written. That single
  fact is the difference between an L and an M, and it is why this bet is takeable at all.

- **#14 is bet, cheaply, to get in front of #16.** Scenarios needs a control-vs-treatment chart for
  PRD-G E3. If the charting decision has not landed when #16 starts, that epic either stalls or
  hand-rolls a chart under delivery pressure — which is how a runtime dependency gets chosen for
  reasons nobody wrote down. An S spike now removes that failure mode. #16's acceptance criteria
  also carry an explicit degrade-to-the-current-table fallback, so the dependency stays soft.

- **Both new gates are enablement, not kill-switches.** `FLAG_RULE_BUILDER_ENABLED` and
  `SCENARIO_AUTHORING_ENABLED` both default **`false`** and are created **DISABLED** in every
  environment. Both add *write* paths onto production control planes — flag definitions that govern
  live evaluation, and fault injection against real targets. Merge dark, verify, flip deliberately.
  Both gate **write controls only**: with the flag off, each page renders its current working view.
  A PM losing the ability to *read* their flags or their running scenarios because a flag flipped is
  a worse failure than losing the ability to author — the same reasoning `app-shell-and-agent-rail`
  used for not gating the section nav.

- **Basis points are the named rabbit hole in both epics.** `rollout.basisPoints` (0–10000) in
  flags, `maxErrorRateBasisPoints` in scenario guardrails. A PM thinks in percent; the conversion
  belongs in the UI and nowhere else. Getting it wrong is a silent 100× targeting error on a
  production flag. This is most of why #15 is risk-high, and it is not the UI.

- **The honesty rail does not soften for #16, it tightens.** Rendering scenario impact as a
  control-vs-treatment chart makes a claim look stronger than the same numbers in a table. The
  "no causal customer claim" and internal/synthetic-cohort caveats (audit §2.6) stay exactly as
  strict, and the product owner reviews the *rendered claim* specifically. On that one story,
  "it looks good" is a warning sign.

- **Two seeds were reclassified `feature` → `spike`, on their own written advice.** Both raw seeds
  said it: #14's said *"a short spike may be the honest first move rather than a build bet"*; #17's
  said *"worth a discovery pass before it is bet."* Scaffolding either as a build epic would have
  been inventing scope the seeds themselves flagged as unvalidated.

## Bookkeeping — the board's ordering was corrected this wave

Not a bet; recorded because it changes how every future wave reads the board.

Two frontmatter fields had been carrying overlapping halves of "what comes next," and
`build-order.mjs` rendered the *wrong one*. `build_order` — documented in `roadmap-to-notion.mjs` as
**epic-README-frontmatter-authoritative** — was empty on **all thirteen** epics, so the SSOT path was
dead code and every value fell through to the seed. Meanwhile the board's `#1`, `#2a` badges came
from `priority`, where `#1` was held by two different epics and `buildOrderNum()` reduced `#2` and
`#2a` to the same `2`, tying the Notion numeric sort.

Corrected: `build_order` is now **the one global sequence**, plain integers, written into every epic
README's frontmatter — `1…12` is the ship history in the order it actually happened, `13…18` the
queue. `priority` now means only *the wave this is slated for*, and `underwritten_by` remains the
record of the wave that actually paid. See `00-ideas/README.md` → *Ordering*.

**One patch is owed to Claude Code**: `build-order.mjs` still renders `r.priority` in its meta line.
Tracked as `seeds/build-order-render-fix.md` (chore, S).

## Why this was worth a wave

The last wave closed the gap between what the landing page promises and what the signed-in product
shows. A PM can now see their agent working. But the two surfaces where the product's actual
differentiation lives — a flag control plane with an immutable version model, and chaos/secops
scenarios that *neither PostHog nor GrowthBook has at all* (audit §3.3) — are still operable only by
an agent with an API credential. The PM who bought the product can read both and act on neither.

Both backends are built, validated, unit-tested and gated. What is missing in each case is a screen.
That is the cheapest kind of gap to close and the most expensive kind to leave open: it is the
difference between owning a whitespace and merely having built one.
