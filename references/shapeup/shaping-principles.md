**Shaping** is the essential pre-work performed on projects before they are considered ready to be scheduled for a development cycle. It is a creative, integrative process—typically conducted by a small senior group—that defines the key elements of a solution at the **right level of abstraction**: concrete enough for teams to know what to do, yet abstract enough to leave room for designers and programmers to work out the details.

### Principles of Shaping
Shaping avoids two extremes: **wireframes**, which are too concrete and stifle creativity, and **words**, which are too abstract and lead to scope creep. Well-shaped work has three primary properties:
*   **It’s rough:** It is clearly unfinished, inviting the team to contribute their expertise.
*   **It’s solved:** Despite being rough, the main elements and macro-level connections are thought through.
*   **It’s bounded:** It clearly indicates what *not* to do and fits within a specific time frame.

### Setting Boundaries
The first step of shaping is defining the **appetite**—the amount of time the organization wants to spend on an idea—rather than asking for a technical estimate. This enforces a **"fixed time, variable scope"** constraint, where the appetite (usually a two-week "Small Batch" or a six-week "Big Batch") acts as a creative limit on the design. During this stage, shapers must **narrow down the problem** by uncovering the specific pain points driving a request to avoid "grab-bag" projects that lack a clear definition of "done".

### Finding the Elements
Once boundaries are set, shapers move quickly to define the solution's elements using high-speed, low-fidelity prototyping techniques:
*   **Breadboarding:** Using a shorthand of **Places** (screens/menus), **Affordances** (buttons/fields), and **Connection lines** to map out the functional flow of a feature without visual styling.
*   **Fat Marker Sketches:** Creating rough sketches with broad strokes (e.g., using a thick-tipped marker) to explore visual arrangements while making it impossible to add premature detail.

### Addressing Risks and Rabbit Holes
After sketching a solution, shapers must "stress-test" it to find **rabbit holes**—technical unknowns or unsolved design problems that could derail a six-week cycle. This involves:
*   **Patching holes:** Making hard design decisions in advance for tricky spots.
*   **Declaring "No Gos":** Explicitly stating which use cases or features are out of bounds to keep the project within the appetite.
*   **Technical vetting:** Presenting the concept to experts to ensure it is feasible to build within the allocated time.

### Writing the Pitch
The final step is formalizing the shaped work into a **pitch** to be presented at the betting table. A successful pitch must include five ingredients:
1.  **Problem:** The specific use case or story motivating the work.
2.  **Appetite:** The time budget (e.g., six weeks).
3.  **Solution:** The core elements, often shown through annotated fat marker sketches or embedded breadboards.
4.  **Rabbit Holes:** Specific warnings about known pitfalls to avoid.
5.  **No-Gos:** Anything intentionally excluded from the project.

This process ensures that by the time a project reaches the **betting table**, it is a well-understood, de-risked option that a team can realistically ship within a single cycle.
