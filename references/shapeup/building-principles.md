In the **Building** phase, responsibility shifts from the shapers to an integrated team of designers and programmers who are granted full autonomy to execute the project within the six-week cycle. The process is defined by several core practices that ensure the team discovers the right tasks and ships on time.

### Handing Over Responsibility
Instead of receiving a list of granular tickets, teams are **assigned whole projects**. They are responsible for defining their own tasks and making the adjustments necessary to fit the work within the cycle's boundaries. In this framework, **"Done" means deployed**; all design, programming, and testing must be completed and released by the end of the six-week window.

### Getting One Piece Done
To avoid the risk of disconnected parts failing to integrate at the end of a project, teams aim to **integrate one "vertical slice"** of the project—connecting UI and backend code—within the first week. 
*   **Affordances over pixels:** Designers provide raw functional elements (buttons, fields) so programmers can start wiring logic before any high-fidelity styling is applied.
*   **Start in the middle:** Teams prioritize work that is **core** to the concept, **small** enough to finish quickly, and **novel** enough to eliminate technical uncertainty early.

### Mapping the Scopes
As the team begins the work, they factor the project into **scopes**: integrated slices of the problem that can be finished independently. 
*   **Discovered, not planned:** Scopes are not created upfront but are identified after the team "walks the territory" and understands the project's natural anatomy.
*   **Categories of work:** Scopes might look like "layer cakes" (simple UI and backend), "icebergs" (complex backend with simple UI), or "chowder" (loose, unrelated tasks).

### Showing Progress with the Hill Chart
To provide status without micromanagement, teams use a **Hill Chart** to visualize their progress. 
*   **Uphill vs. Downhill:** The uphill side represents the phase of **figuring out** the approach and solving unknowns, while the downhill side represents the certain phase of **execution**. 
*   **Status without asking:** A dot that doesn't move on the hill acts as a "raised hand," signaling to managers that a scope is stuck on an unknown without the team having to admit failure.

### Deciding When to Stop
Because time is fixed, **scope is variable**. Teams must be disciplined about **"scope hammering"**—repeatedly questioning if a task is a "must-have" or a "nice-to-have" (the latter are marked with a **~** and usually cut). To determine if a feature is ready, teams **compare it to the baseline** (the customer's current reality) rather than an unreachable ideal. 

### Moving On
Once a project ships, the team is encouraged to **let the "storm" of initial feedback pass**. New requests or bugs are treated as **raw ideas** for the shaping track rather than immediate obligations. By refusing to commit to immediate changes, the organization stays **debt-free** and maintains a clean slate for the next betting cycle.
